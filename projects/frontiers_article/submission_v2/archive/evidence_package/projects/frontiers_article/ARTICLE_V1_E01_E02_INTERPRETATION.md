This note records the scientific interpretation of E01 and E02 after the literature and analytic audits. It distinguishes:

1. what the frozen experiments actually showed;

# E01 — Restricted Feedback and Early Rejection

## 1. Scientific question

E01 asks whether an early adverse outcome from authenticity can alter subsequent behavior when the learner observes only the outcomes generated by its own choices.

The relevant mechanism is endogenous sampling: choosing an action determines whether the learner obtains further information about it.

## 2. Frozen experimental result

Across five master seeds, an initial rejected authentic outcome:

- reduced subsequent authentic sampling by approximately `0.025837`;
- increased normalized regret by approximately `0.025824`;
- did **not** produce a detectable persistent increase in final masking: the pooled final-window masking contrast was approximately `-0.000343`, with the uncertainty interval spanning zero.

Within the rejection stratum, forced authentic exploration and counterfactual feedback reduced final masking and regret.

The experimentally supported chain is therefore:

```text
early rejection
    ↓
reduced subsequent sampling of authenticity
    ↓
more suboptimal choices during learning
    ↓
higher cumulative regret
```

The frozen evidence does **not** support:

```text
early rejection
    ↓
persistent final masking
```

## 3. Meaning of regret in E01

Regret is a learning-performance quantity, not a psychological feeling. It measures the objective reward lost because the learner does not consistently choose the objectively better action.

Conceptually,

\[
R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[V_{\mathrm{best}}-V(a_t)\right].
\]

If authenticity is objectively preferable, an early rejection can temporarily make the learner choose masking more often. Those choices forgo objective reward. Even if the learner later recovers and its final behavior resembles the control condition, the earlier lost reward remains in cumulative regret.

Thus, higher regret and a null final-mask contrast are entirely compatible:

```text
trajectory is worse
but
final policy is not detectably different
```

## 4. Is the result theoretically expected?

Yes. E01 reproduces an established hot-stove/endogenous-sampling mechanism.

The generic logic is:

1. an early adverse outcome reduces the estimated value of an action;
2. the action is sampled less frequently;
3. reduced sampling limits corrective evidence;
4. the erroneous pessimistic estimate can therefore persist for a substantial finite period.

This is consistent with hot-stove effects, self-confirming incomplete learning, and finite-horizon softmax learning. Information-restoring interventions are also theoretically expected to help because they break the avoidance → missing-information loop.

Therefore E01 is a **baseline/supporting demonstration**, not a new formal learning mechanism.

## 5. Should persistent suboptimal masking occur under some finite-horizon conditions?

### 5.1 Pure hot-stove case: objective and learner rewards aligned

With `lambda = 1`, the learner values rewards on the same scale as the environment. Under the frozen M0 reward parameters, authenticity is objectively preferable when

\[
p > p^*_{\mathrm{obj}} = \frac{43}{60} \approx 0.7167.
\]

An early rejection can still drive the learned value of authenticity downward. Fixed-temperature softmax continues to assign authenticity positive probability, so the state is not absorbing. However, corrective sampling can become rare enough that the learner remains behaviorally biased toward masking for a reasonably long finite horizon.

The most vulnerable region should be near the objective boundary:

\[
p \gtrsim \frac{43}{60},
\]

because authenticity is objectively better there, but only by a small margin. A severe early negative sample can dominate that small advantage for many subsequent updates.

Theoretical expectation:

```text
small objective authenticity advantage
+ early adverse sample
+ limited finite horizon
+ low effective exploration
→ prolonged suboptimal masking is plausible
```

But the frozen E01 evidence does **not** establish a validated parameter subregion in which an early rejection creates persistent final masking. The overall final-window contrast was null.

### 5.2 Learner/objective mismatch: `lambda > 1`

A separate mechanism exists when negative observed rewards are overweighted by the learner.

The exact learner-side crossover is

\[
p^*_{\lambda}
=
\frac{0.75+1.4\lambda}{1.6+1.4\lambda}.
\]

For `lambda > 1`,

\[
p^*_{\lambda} > p^*_{\mathrm{obj}}.
\]

Therefore the interval

\[
\boxed{
p^*_{\mathrm{obj}} < p < p^*_{\lambda}
}
\]

has a precise interpretation:

- authenticity has higher **objective environmental value**;
- masking has higher **expected transformed reward for the learner**.

For example, at `lambda = 2`,

\[
p^*_{\lambda} \approx 0.8068,
\]

so the interval

\[
0.7167 < p < 0.8068
\]

is objectively authenticity-favoring while learner-subjectively masking-favoring.

Sustained suboptimal masking is therefore expected there, but this is **not** a pure hot-stove information trap. It is an objective/subjective valuation mismatch induced by the learner definition.

## 6. Final interpretation of E01

E01 establishes that early rejection can alter the learning trajectory even when it does not produce a persistent final behavioral state.

The strongest accurate summary is:

> In the frozen M0 experiment, an initial rejected authentic action reduced subsequent authentic sampling and increased cumulative regret, while producing no detectable persistent increase in final masking. This is consistent with established finite-horizon hot-stove/endogenous-sampling theory. Persistent suboptimal masking is theoretically plausible near weak objective advantages under sufficiently limited exploration and horizon, but the frozen E01 experiment did not identify such a persistent-mask region.

## 7. Article-V1 claim boundary for E01

### Supported

- Early rejection reduces subsequent authentic sampling in the tested domain.
- Early rejection increases normalized regret in the tested domain.
- Information-restoring interventions reduce the resulting learning cost in the tested rejection stratum.
- Greater learner-side negative-reward weighting can increase masking while objective environmental rewards remain unchanged.

### Not supported

- One rejection causes persistent final masking.
- E01 identifies an irreversible masking trap.
- The hot-stove mechanism is novel.
- `lambda` is a calibrated human psychological parameter.
- A small persistent-mask parameter region has already been demonstrated by E01.

# E02 — Graded Disclosure and Endpoint Discovery

## 1. Scientific question

E02 asks what happens when a binary conceal/authentic choice is replaced by multiple graded disclosure levels.

The key scientific issue is whether graded actions create a meaningful disclosure-specific mechanism or primarily alter the learning problem by expanding the number and representation of actions.

## 2. Exact objective structure

M1 uses disclosure level

\[
d \in [0,1],
\]

with:

- `d = 0`: full concealment;
- `d = 1`: full visibility/authenticity;
- intermediate `d`: partial disclosure.

Under the frozen M1 model,

\[
E[R(d)] = 0.75 + d(3p-2.15).
\]

The objective is therefore affine in `d`.

Consequently:

\[
p > \frac{43}{60}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
d=1\text{ is the unique static optimum},
\]

and

\[
p < \frac{43}{60}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
d=0\text{ is the unique static optimum}.
\]

At exact indifference,

\[
p=\frac{43}{60},
\]

every disclosure level has equal objective value.

Thus, except at exact indifference, there is **no objectively optimal intermediate disclosure level** in the maintained model.

## 3. Frozen experimental result

Relative to the binary architecture, the five-level graded architecture:

- increased continued nonzero-disclosure sampling after rejection by approximately `+0.255040`;
- reduced full-authentic final-window behavior by approximately `-0.328692`;
- reduced the probability that the objectively optimal endpoint was the modal action by approximately `-0.221740`;
- did not improve pooled regret under the frozen sign convention.

The observed pattern is therefore:

```text
graded disclosure
    ↓
less complete withdrawal to d = 0
but also
less discovery/use of d = 1
```

The graded action space keeps behavior away from the extremes, but this does not imply that the middle is objectively better.

## 4. Are intermediate actions objectively suboptimal?

When `p > 43/60`, full authenticity is objectively optimal. For any `d < 1`,

\[
E[R(1)]-E[R(d)]
=
(1-d)(3p-2.15).
\]

Thus every intermediate level is objectively suboptimal whenever authenticity is strictly optimal.

For example, at `p = 0.75`,

\[
3p-2.15 = 0.10.
\]

The objective losses relative to `d = 1` are:

| Disclosure level | Expected loss relative to `d=1` |
|---|---:|
| `1.00` | `0` |
| `0.75` | `0.025` |
| `0.50` | `0.050` |
| `0.25` | `0.075` |
| `0.00` | `0.100` |

Intermediate disclosure can therefore appear behaviorally moderate while remaining objectively inferior.

The same logic works in the opposite direction below the boundary. If `p < 43/60`, then `d = 0` is objectively optimal and persistent nonzero disclosure is itself suboptimal.

E02 is therefore not inherently a “masking trap” experiment. It is an **endpoint-identification experiment**.

## 5. Why should theory expect persistent intermediate behavior?

The frozen learner treats each disclosure level as an independent tabular action.

For example,

\[
d \in \{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}.
\]

Learning about `d = 0.75` does not directly update the estimate of `d = 1`, even though the actions are semantically and mathematically ordered.

This produces two generic learning effects.

### 5.1 Sampling dilution

With two independent actions, the learner allocates its experience between two value estimates.

With five independent actions, the same finite horizon is divided among five estimates. The optimal endpoint can therefore receive fewer corrective observations.

### 5.2 Intermediate actions can look safer

In M1, the acceptance probability is

\[
q(d)=1-(1-p)d.
\]

As disclosure increases, rejection becomes more likely. Intermediate levels can therefore accumulate less volatile or less adverse finite histories than full authenticity, even when full authenticity has the highest expected objective reward.

A finite-horizon learner can consequently maintain a temporary ordering such as

\[
Q(0.5)>Q(0.75)>Q(1)
\]

while the true expected rewards satisfy

\[
E[R(1)]>E[R(0.75)]>E[R(0.5)].
\]

Softmax then samples the optimal endpoint less often, which slows correction.

This is consistent with generic multi-arm sampling and action-representation theory. It does not require a disclosure-specific psychological mechanism.

## 6. Where should the effect be strongest?

The obvious vulnerable region is again near the objective boundary:

\[
p \gtrsim \frac{43}{60}.
\]

The reward difference between adjacent levels separated by `Delta d` is

\[
\Delta E[R] = \Delta d\,(3p-2.15).
\]

For five equally spaced levels, `Delta d = 0.25`.

At `p = 0.72`,

\[
3p-2.15 = 0.01,
\]

so adjacent actions differ by only

\[
0.25 \times 0.01 = 0.0025
\]

in expected reward.

This is extremely small relative to stochastic outcome variation. It should therefore be difficult for a finite-horizon tabular learner to reliably distinguish `d = 0.5`, `0.75`, and `1`.

Farther from the boundary, the endpoint advantage becomes larger and easier to learn. For example, at `p = 0.9`,

\[
3p-2.15 = 0.55,
\]

so adjacent levels differ by

\[
0.25 \times 0.55 = 0.1375.
\]

The expected qualitative pattern is therefore:

```text
near objective indifference
+ several independent graded actions
+ finite horizon
→ difficulty identifying the correct endpoint
→ persistent intermediate behavior becomes more likely
```

This expectation follows from the exact M1 reward geometry plus generic finite-sample learning theory.

## 7. Does E02 demonstrate persistent suboptimal partial disclosure?

More directly than E01, yes — but only in a bounded sense.

The frozen E02 evidence shows end-of-horizon displacement away from full authenticity under the graded architecture, including reduced full-authentic final-window behavior and reduced probability that the optimal endpoint is modal.

Therefore E02 does demonstrate that introducing independent intermediate actions can produce persistent finite-horizon displacement away from the optimal endpoint in the tested design.

However, the current authority does **not** establish a formal parameter region of the form

\[
p \in [p_1,p_2]
\Rightarrow
\text{persistent intermediate disclosure}.
\]

Nor does it isolate a disclosure-specific mechanism from generic independent-arm sample dilution and lack of generalization across neighboring actions.

## 8. Symmetry of the E02 error

The experiment's conceptual symmetry is important.

When

\[
p > 43/60,
\]

full authenticity is optimal, so persistent intermediate disclosure means **too little disclosure**.

When

\[
p < 43/60,
\]

full concealment is optimal, so persistent intermediate disclosure means **too much disclosure**.

Thus the fundamental phenomenon is not specifically masking. It is:

> graded independent actions can make finite-horizon endpoint identification harder.

This is also why the literature audit treats generic action representation as a serious alternative explanation for E02.

## 9. Final interpretation of E02

The strongest accurate summary is:

> In the frozen M1 experiment, adding independent intermediate disclosure levels preserved more nonzero disclosure after rejection but reduced discovery and use of the objectively optimal full-authentic endpoint and did not improve welfare. Because the M1 objective is affine and endpoint-optimal, the observed intermediate behavior is a learning/action-representation effect rather than evidence for an intrinsically optimal compromise level. Generic finite-sample theory predicts the effect should be strongest near the objective boundary, where reward differences among adjacent disclosure levels are very small.

## 10. Article-V1 claim boundary for E02

### Supported

- Five independent graded disclosure actions preserve more nonzero-disclosure sampling than binary endpoints in the frozen architecture.
- The graded architecture reduces full-authentic endpoint discovery/use in the tested design.
- Grading does not improve pooled regret/welfare in the frozen experiment.
- Intermediate actions alter learning and exposure while leaving the static objective endpoint-optimal.
- Persistent finite-horizon displacement away from the optimal endpoint occurs in the tested graded architecture.

### Not supported

- Intermediate disclosure is objectively optimal.
- Graded disclosure improves welfare.
- Graded disclosure improves discovery of full authenticity.
- The E02 effect is a disclosure-specific psychological mechanism.
- The effect would survive a learner that generalizes across neighboring disclosure levels.
- A formal persistent-intermediate-disclosure parameter region has already been established.

# E01 versus E02

| Dimension | E01 | E02 |
|---|---|---|
| Experimental manipulation | Early adverse outcome under restricted feedback | Replace binary actions with graded independent actions |
| Main finite-horizon effect | Reduced later authentic sampling | More continued intermediate/nonzero disclosure |
| Final endpoint distortion | Not detected overall | Detected: reduced full-authentic endpoint behavior |
| Regret/welfare | Early rejection increases regret | Grading does not improve welfare |
| Exact objective structure | Binary objective threshold | Same endpoint threshold; intermediate levels never strictly optimal |
| Generic theoretical explanation | Hot-stove / endogenous sampling | Sample dilution + independent-arm representation |
| Persistent suboptimal behavior | Theoretically plausible but not demonstrated as a final-mask region | Demonstrated as end-of-horizon displacement from the optimal endpoint in the tested architecture |
| Article-V1 role | Background/baseline mechanism demonstration | Supporting, representation-sensitive demonstration |

## Combined scientific lesson

E01 and E02 separate two different ways finite-horizon learning can fail without changing the objective optimum.

E01 shows that **history changes sampling**:

```text
early adverse evidence
→ reduced sampling
→ delayed correction
→ cumulative objective cost
```

E02 shows that **action representation changes identification**:

```text
more independently represented compromise actions
→ experience distributed across more value estimates
→ continued intermediate behavior
→ poorer endpoint identification
```

Neither result establishes a novel general reinforcement-learning mechanism. Their Article-V1 value is to define the baseline learning phenomena against which the structurally richer E03 and E04 results must be interpreted.
