## Frozen Abstract (221 words)

Gifted learners may reduce the visibility of ability in social settings, yet
similar concealment need not reflect the same learner–environment relationship.
Observed behaviour alone may therefore underidentify whether lower visibility
reflects current social conditions, learned expectations, the available action
representation, or difficulty recognizing receptive alternatives.

We used five controlled reinforcement-learning experiments with finite
learners to separate the effects of social-feedback history, independently
learned disclosure options, persistent social consequences, contextual
discoverability, and learned versus environmental decision boundaries. Each
experiment compares learned actions with the stipulated payoff structure or,
where relevant, a full-information dynamic benchmark.

An adverse initial authentic outcome reduced subsequent authentic sampling
without a detected persistent final-window masking effect. Independently
learned graded options sustained non-zero disclosure but reduced selection of
the full-authentic endpoint. When actions changed future social state, learned
masking diverged from the full-information dynamic optimum in some tested
conditions. An available receptive context became less discoverable as the
contextual search problem expanded under particular action representations. In
the comparison-cost experiment, the learned behavioural crossing was 0.313,
below the stipulated environmental crossover of 0.471.

These controlled models show why concealment cannot uniquely identify current
adaptation, learned expectation, or the discoverability of receptive
opportunities. Empirical studies should measure current social conditions,
feedback history, expectations, behavioural expression, subsequent sampling,
and contextual access separately rather than infer them from concealment
alone.
