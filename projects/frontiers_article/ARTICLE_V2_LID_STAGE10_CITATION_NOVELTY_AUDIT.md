manuscript evidence freeze, inferential audit, experiment interpretation notes,

for Authors. The bounded search addressed only the questions left open by the
repository authorities. Absence from this search is not treated as universal

## 4. Giftedness and social-coping source check

### 4.1 What the retained sources establish

- Coleman and Cross (1988) studied 15 gifted adolescents and directly grounded
  the social-handicap/information-management framing.
- Cross, Coleman, and Stewart (1993) examined the stigma-of-giftedness
  paradigm and social cognition.
- Swiatek (1995) empirically examined multiple social-coping strategies rather
  than a single hiding construct.
- Cross et al. (2019) documented cross-cultural variation in the social
  experience and visibility of giftedness.
- Klimecká (2023) reported heterogeneous perceived advantages,
  disadvantages, and coping responses in a Czech sample.
- Lavrijsen and Verschueren (2023) and Tourreix et al. (2023) directly resist a
  uniform maladjustment narrative.

### 4.2 What they do not establish

They do not establish that gifted learners generally conceal ability, a
population prevalence of concealment, a universal developmental pathway,
systematic sequential search for receptive contexts, or the article's
reinforcement-learning mechanisms in humans. The current manuscript does not
make those claims.

## 7. Underidentification, equifinality, and individual differences

The closest established principle is equifinality: multiple pathways or
initial conditions can lead to a similar endpoint. Cicchetti and Rogosch's
(1996) primary editorial defines and develops this principle in developmental
psychopathology. The general proposition is therefore not novel.

V2's narrower contribution is the application to gifted ability concealment
and the explicit computational decomposition of five routes: sampled feedback
history, independent action representation, persistent social state,
receptive-context discovery, and learned/objective boundary displacement.

**Classification:** NECESSARY_POSITIONING. One sentence was added to §1.2.
The phrase underidentified behavioural phenotype remains useful because the
article concerns inference from an observed behaviour, but it is not presented
as invention of equifinality.

## 8. Environmental legibility

### 8.1 Adjacent constructs checked

| Adjacent construct | Overlap | Non-equivalence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| affordance/opportunity availability | Whether an environment offers possible action | Does not ensure the learner can identify which option is receptive before sampling. |
| psychological safety | Shared or perceived safety for interpersonal risk taking | Describes experienced climate/safety, not awareness, search burden, or identification accuracy across alternatives. |
| school/classroom climate and perceived support | Current support, relationships, fairness, or safety | Measures the quality of a setting more directly than discoverability of an unknown receptive setting. |
| person–environment fit | Compatibility between person and setting | Fit can exist even when the learner cannot locate or recognize the setting. |
| school belonging | Felt membership/acceptance | Usually assessed after or within a setting; it does not separate existence, awareness, and access. |
| architectural environmental legibility | Cognitive mapping and wayfinding through physical layouts | Shares the idea of recognizability but concerns navigation in built space, not social receptivity. |

Primary anchors included Edmondson (1999), “Psychological Safety and Learning
Behavior in Work Teams,” *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350–383,
https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999; Roberts and Robins (2004),
“Person–Environment Fit and Its Implications for Personality Development,”
*Journal of Personality, 72*(1), 89–110,
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0022-3506.2004.00257.x; and O'Neill (1991),
“Evaluation of a Conceptual Model of Architectural Legibility,” *Environment
and Behavior, 23*(3), 259–284,
https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916591233001. Abstract and publisher metadata were
inspected.

### 8.2 Decision

**PARTIAL_OVERLAP.** No close established equivalent was found that jointly
captures:

1. a receptive option's existence;
2. the learner's awareness of it;
3. expected receptivity before costly sampling;
4. access/search cost; and
5. accuracy of identifying the receptive option.

The manuscript already describes environmental legibility as model-derived and
states that its relation to established constructs and empirical
operationalization remain open. **Main-manuscript gate: NO_CHANGE.**

## 9. Graded disclosure and action representation

Graded and partial self-disclosure are established topics. For example, Niwa
and Maruno (2010), “Development of a Scale to Assess the Depth of
Self-disclosure,” *The Japanese Journal of Personality, 18*(3), 196–209,
https://doi.org/10.2132/personality.18.196, operationalized four disclosure
depths. Social penetration theory likewise treats disclosure depth and breadth
as graded.

The bounded search did not find a prior gifted-education or disclosure result
matching E02's exact conjunction: independently learned ordered disclosure
arms increase subsequent non-zero disclosure while reducing discovery/use of
an objectively optimal endpoint. Related structured-bandit work instead
underscores that information can generalize across related actions when the
model represents that structure; E02 deliberately does not.

**Conclusion:** graded disclosure is established; generic action-set dilution
and representation dependence are not novel; the ability-concealment
application and the measured paired trade-off are V2-specific. The manuscript
already states the independent-arm limitation and makes no universal novelty
claim. **Gate: NO_CHANGE.**

## 10. Persistent social state and reputation

Chaudoir and Fisher's (2010) Disclosure Processes Model is directly relevant
prior art. It treats disclosure as a process involving antecedent goals, the
disclosure event, individual/dyadic/social-contextual outcomes, and feedback
into later disclosure decisions. It therefore establishes that disclosure and
its consequences can be dynamic.

The bounded search did not identify a prior gifted ability-concealment MDP
with the article's Neutral/Accepted/Stigmatized transition kernel or its
finite-learner versus full-information dynamic-policy comparison. That absence
is bounded, not universal.

**Conclusion:** E03 does not invent dynamic disclosure or reputation
consequences. Its contribution is a model-specific gifted-concealment
application that separates immediate reward, dynamic value, and learned/oracle
disagreement. **Classification: NECESSARY_POSITIONING.** One sentence was
added to §4.4.

Primary source inspected in full text:
Chaudoir, S. R., & Fisher, J. D. (2010). The disclosure processes model:
Understanding disclosure decision making and postdisclosure outcomes among
people living with a concealable stigmatized identity. *Psychological
Bulletin, 136*(2), 236–256. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018193

## 11. Receptive-context discovery and niche selection

Adjacent work establishes that people select or shape environments and that
fit and peer context matter. Scarr and McCartney (1983), “How People Make Their
Own Environments: A Theory of Genotype–Environment Effects,” *Child
Development, 54*(2), 424–435, https://doi.org/10.2307/1129703, is a classic
niche-picking account. Gifted peer research also supports context-dependent
acceptance; for example, Verschueren, Lavrijsen, Weyns, Ramos, and De Fraine
(2019), “Social Acceptance of High-Ability Youth: Multiple Perspectives and
Contextual Influences,” *New Directions for Child and Adolescent Development,
2019*(168), 27–46,
https://doi.org/10.1002/cad.20316, found that perceived acceptance varied with
class context. Abstract/metadata were inspected.

These literatures establish selection, fit, and contextual variation more
strongly than finite discovery of one unknown receptive option among
alternatives. E04's contribution is the controlled separation of objective
availability from finite discoverability under four explicit representations.
It does not model literal movement or claim a general context-count law.

**Conclusion:** PARTIAL_OVERLAP; no main-text change is required because §1.5,
§2.5, §4.5, and Limitations already state the bounded distinction. Candidate
niche-selection citations are OPTIONAL_ENRICHMENT and were not inserted.

## 12. Empirical measurement programme

Existing instruments cover several components of the proposed programme, but
no one instrument identifies the model mechanisms. Measures should be
combined with repeated behavioural and event-linked data.

| Domain | Candidate instrument/operationalization | What it measures | Fit and remaining gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| gifted ability visibility/social coping | Revised Social Coping Questionnaire: Swiatek & Dorr (1998), *Journal of Secondary Gifted Education, 10*(1), 252–259, https://doi.org/10.1177/1932202X9801000105 | Denial, popularity, peer acceptance, social interaction, hiding giftedness | Strong gifted-specific strategy measure; not a direct time series of expression or objective environment. |
| observed visibility | repeated context-coded observations or event-contingent reports of advanced answers, interest disclosure, requests for challenge, selective participation, and concealment | Behaviour by setting and occasion | Needed to distinguish expression from retrospective strategy endorsement. |
| learned social expectations | Children's Rejection Sensitivity Questionnaire from Downey, Lebolt, Rincón, & Freitas (1998), *Child Development, 69*(4), 1074–1091, https://doi.org/10.2307/1132363; plus scenario-specific predicted peer/teacher responses | Anxious/angry expectations and reactions to rejection | General rejection expectation is relevant but not identical to predicted consequences of ability visibility; task-specific expectations are needed. |
| feedback history | longitudinal event-contingent diary linking each visibility action, peer/teacher response, and subsequent choice | Actual sequence of acceptance/rejection and resampling | Preferred over a sole retrospective global scale; requires burden-aware design and corroborating observations where feasible. |
| peer acceptance | sociometric nominations/ratings following Coie, Dodge, & Coppotelli (1982), *Developmental Psychology, 18*(4), 557–570, https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.18.4.557 | Peer social preference/status | Measures current relational outcome, not learner expectation or discoverability. |

| school/classroom receptivity | Delaware School Climate Survey–Student: Bear, Gaskins, Blank, & Chen (2011), *Journal of School Psychology, 49*(2), 157–174, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2011.01.001 | Teacher-student and student-student relations, fairness, liking, safety | Strong current-climate measure; should be paired with ability-expression-specific contingencies. |
| academic support climate | Academic School Climate Scale for Students: Spring & Ochoa (2024), *Journal of Psychoeducational Assessment, 42*(5), 483–497, https://doi.org/10.1177/07342829241249353 | High standards, teacher support of learning, peer support of learning | Relevant to receptivity for challenge; does not measure whether opportunities are known or findable. |

These candidates are a future empirical design resource. They were not added
to the manuscript, because doing so would convert §4.7 into a measure-review
subsection and exceed the Stage 10 change gate.
