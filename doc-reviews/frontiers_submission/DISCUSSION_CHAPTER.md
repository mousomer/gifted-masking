# Discussion: How Risk, Search, and Feedback Make Masking Persist

The central finding is that persistent masking can be learned even when authenticity is the objectively superior policy. This was not a narrow boundary effect. Across the maintained M2 grid, 226 of 357 tested receptivity-by-escape cells (63.3%) were classified as learned suboptimal masking, and the mean learned neutral-state masking probability within those cells was 0.950. The learner repeatedly selected the safe action in large portions of an environment where value iteration favored authenticity. The result establishes a mechanism-level possibility that is absent from trait accounts of concealment: a learner can become persistently hidden from opportunities that are already worth taking because earlier adverse experience restricts access to later corrective evidence.

The model cascade shows how this basic failure is enlarged, reduced, or redirected. Negative-outcome weighting deepened avoidance without changing objective rewards. Sticky stigma prolonged the consequences of rejection but was not required for learned masking to arise. Graded disclosure created lower-exposure routes for continued sampling. Context search made a rare receptive setting harder to discover, but only when the action architecture imposed a genuine search burden. A sufficiently large comparison cost eliminated the value of authenticity in an otherwise receptive context. Endogenous receptivity then closed the loop: masking changed the future environment and produced different outcomes at the same current parameter value after different histories. Together, the models replace a single broad explanation—“the student prefers to hide”—with a family of distinguishable learning problems.

## Persistent masking is a policy outcome, not evidence of a fixed disposition

The first theoretical contribution is to relocate persistence from personality to the interaction between experience and sampling. The stigma-of-giftedness and social-coping literature shows that some gifted students manage the visibility of ability through denial, conformity, deflection, humor, helping, or selective disclosure (Coleman and Cross, 1988; Swiatek, 1995; Cross et al., 2019). Those findings establish behavioral diversity and contextual sensitivity. They do not require the assumption that gifted students are generally risk averse, socially impaired, or intrinsically inclined toward concealment.

M0 demonstrates why such an assumption is unnecessary. Authenticity produces variable outcomes, whereas masking delivers a comparatively predictable return. A negative authentic outcome lowers its learned value; the learner then selects it less often; and reduced sampling limits access to evidence that the action can succeed. This is the hot-stove mechanism described by March (1996) and Denrell and March (2001), applied to the visibility of giftedness. Apparent risk aversion emerges from adaptive sampling even when no globally risk-averse trait has been specified.

The distinction matters because the same observed mask rate can arise for different reasons. Masking may be objectively valuable in an unreceptive environment. It may be a temporary response while a learner gathers evidence. It may reflect a pessimistic learned estimate that persists after the environment has become favorable. Or it may be sustained by a search or feedback process that keeps favorable evidence inaccessible. Behavioral description alone cannot distinguish these cases. The analytic thresholds and optimal-policy comparisons in this study make that distinction explicit.

“Objectively optimal” must also be interpreted narrowly. It means optimal under the model's stated rewards and transition probabilities, not morally superior for every student or situation. Selective disclosure can protect privacy, preserve safety, or express a legitimate preference. The result is not that students should always be authentic. It is that the same outward behavior can remain in place after its modeled instrumental advantage has disappeared. That possibility makes history and counterfactual information central to interpretation.

## Hot-stove information restriction is the central learning mechanism

The strongest evidence for information restriction came from M2. At \(p=0.55\), authenticity was the optimal neutral-state action both when stigma escape was impossible and when \(\eta=0.20\). Learned neutral-state masking nevertheless reached 0.984 and 0.973, with objective regrets of 3.472 and 3.575. The learner's behavior was therefore compared against the computed optimum of the same environment, not against an informal expectation that authenticity ought to be better.

The phase-grid result shows that the failure is not confined to a knife-edge region. The maintained dynamic authenticity threshold ranged from 0.3839 at \(\eta=0\) to 0.2690 at \(\eta=0.80\), yet learned masking occupied many cells with substantially greater receptivity. Acceptance was dynamically valuable because it opened a reliably rewarding state. The learner often failed to exploit that long-run advantage because adverse samples depressed authenticity before its future value was adequately learned.

Exploration sensitivity supports this interpretation. At \(p=0.75\) and \(\eta=0\), lower exploration raised learned neutral-state masking from the default 0.950 to 0.994, whereas higher exploration reduced it to 0.649. Increasing access to the disfavored action weakened persistence by 0.301 relative to the default condition. This is the direction predicted by information restriction: the more often authenticity continues to be sampled, the more opportunity the learner has to discover its favorable returns.

The mechanism should nevertheless be described with causal precision. Exploration sensitivity and the model's update structure strongly implicate restricted sampling, but the maintained suite does not directly restore counterfactual information while holding action frequencies otherwise controlled. A forced-exploration, replay, or observational-learning ablation would provide a sharper test. If supplying corrective evidence substantially reduces masking without changing objective rewards, the information-restriction account would be strengthened. If it does not, persistence must be attributed more heavily to value-update dynamics, state occupancy, or another feature of the learner.

## Negative weighting and sticky stigma deepen different parts of the process

Negative-outcome weighting and stigma persistence both increased masking, but they did so at different levels of explanation. Raising \(\lambda\) from 0.50 to 2.50 increased masking by 0.403 at fixed receptivity. Because \(\lambda\) changed only the subjective learning signal, not the environment's objective reward, it altered how much a negative event moved the learner's estimate. Sticky stigma changed the environment after rejection: it prolonged the period in which future outcomes remained less favorable.

This separation prevents a common interpretive error. A learner who reacts strongly to a negative event and a learner facing genuinely lasting social consequences may behave similarly, but they do not face the same problem. The first mechanism suggests sensitivity to experience; the second suggests persistence in environmental state. Empirical work that measures only later masking cannot identify which process operated. It must also measure the experienced consequence, its perceived severity, whether peer treatment changed, and whether a fresh context reset the opportunity structure.

The M2 escape contrast illustrates the difference. Increasing \(\eta\) from 0 to 0.20 reduced final-window masking by 0.201, a 25.6% decrease relative to the no-escape condition. Environmental recovery therefore mattered substantially. Yet neutral-state mask probabilities remained above 0.97 in both cells. Removing some persistence from the environment did not automatically remove the learned avoidance policy. Once authenticity had been undersampled, a better current state did not guarantee behavioral recovery.

This finding revises the strongest version of a “structural lock-in” claim. Under the default transition structure, the dominant phenomenon was learner failure relative to the optimal MDP policy, not an environment in which an optimal planner should mask. Genuine optimal-policy masking above the static threshold appeared only in a narrow harsher regime, with a largest tested width of 0.0563. Structural lock-in is possible, but parameter-contingent. Learned persistence is the broader result.

## Graded disclosure separates exploratory value from endpoint value

M1 adds an important qualification to binary accounts of visibility. The social-coping literature documents a continuum that includes direct expression, placating, deflection, partial concealment, and denial (Cross et al., 2014). The model translates that continuum into graded disclosure actions. Its central insight is that an action can be useful for exploration even when it is not the highest-value final policy.

The expected-reward function in M1 is linear, so intermediate disclosure is generally not a new static optimum. Its value lies in limiting exposure during sampling. A learner who no longer selects full authenticity may still test the environment through a lower-visibility contribution. Such actions maintain contact with informative outcomes and can prevent the evidence stream from closing completely.

The observed disclosure contrast was large: mean final-window disclosure increased by 0.569 between the low- and high-receptivity conditions. This confirms that the graded learner responded strongly to environmental receptivity. It does not yet quantify how much better a graded action space preserves exploration than an otherwise identical binary one. The current maintained result compares receptive conditions within M1, while the stronger discussion claim compares action architectures. A direct binary-versus-graded ablation is therefore the next required test.

The educational implication is a hypothesis about opportunity design, not a prescription for managed self-concealment. Low-stakes contributions, limited-audience disclosure, interest-based groups, or graduated participation may preserve a route toward fuller expression after highly visible participation has become too risky. Whether such routes help depends on transfer. The implemented learner assigns independent values to each disclosure level, so success at one level does not generalize automatically to another. Human learners may generalize more, less, or asymmetrically depending on similarity, context, and trust.

## Context search—not context count—creates the multidimensional burden

M3 produced one of the clearest mechanism identifications in the study. Increasing the number of contexts reduced discovery of good-context authenticity in flat and hierarchical representations, but the effect nearly disappeared when masking was represented globally or the good context was fixed and known. The \(K=1\) minus \(K=8\) gap was 0.168 in the flat ablation and 0.377 in the hierarchical model, compared with 0.005 under global masking and -0.005 when the good context was known.

The conclusion is therefore not that social variety is harmful. More contexts became burdensome when they multiplied context-specific alternatives through which one rare receptive option had to be found. The active variable was the organization of search. Masking remained a broadly available safe response, whereas authentic success required the correct conjunction of context and behavior. When the model compressed masking into one global action or supplied the receptive context, context number lost its effect.

This result extends person-environment-fit accounts by adding discoverability. A compatible peer setting has limited behavioral value if the learner cannot identify it, cannot transfer safety information into it, or must sample many unreceptive alternatives to find it. The finding aligns with evidence that social acceptance and coping vary across classrooms, programs, and cultures (Cross and Swiatek, 2009; Cross et al., 2019; Verschueren et al., 2019), while explaining why the existence of a receptive group may not translate into visible participation.

Environmental legibility is the resulting theoretical construct. It refers to how clearly a setting signals who is receptive, how costly it is to test those signals, and whether learning transfers across apparently similar contexts. The model predicts that increasing the number of social opportunities will help only when learners can recognize or efficiently test which opportunities are safe. Clear norms, reliable adult guidance, stable group membership, and transferable signals of acceptance should reduce the search burden. These remain empirical predictions, not demonstrated intervention effects.

## Comparison costs prompted retreat before objective indifference

The comparison-cost result shows that acceptance is not the only determinant of a context's value, but its main contribution is sharper than the endpoint contrast. Analytically, receptive-context authenticity remained objectively preferable to masking until \(bf=8/17=0.4706\). In the frozen flat-2K learner, however, the learned population response crossed below 0.5 at \(bf=0.313\) (95% master-seed bootstrap interval 0.308 to 0.317). The learner therefore retreated from authenticity while that action still retained an objective advantage of approximately 0.134.

This learned-objective separation identifies a regime of premature retreat within the model. It is consistent with a hot-stove process: costly or negative samples depress an action's learned value, reduced selection then limits corrective experience, and the safe response dominates before the risky alternative loses its expected value. E05 does not uniquely prove that causal account, however. Finite horizon, softmax exploration, independent joint-action values, and competition among eight arms also contribute to the tested learning problem. Isolating hot-stove persistence would require forced-exploration, counterfactual-feedback, or otherwise mechanism-removing comparisons.

This mechanism provides a bridge to the big-fish-little-pond literature. Academic self-concept is shaped partly by comparison with the achievement level of the surrounding group, and meta-analytic evidence supports a negative contextual association on average (Marsh and Hau, 2003; Fang et al., 2018). Yet research on gifted grouping also shows heterogeneous outcomes: a high-ability setting can create comparison costs while improving challenge, belonging, achievement, or access to intellectual peers (Preckel et al., 2010, 2019).

The model should therefore not be read as evidence against high-ability grouping. Its narrower contribution is to show that receptivity and comparison are distinct dimensions. A context may be welcoming and intellectually appropriate while still imposing a self-evaluative cost; conversely, a low-comparison setting may remain socially unreceptive. Educational design must consider both rather than treating peer similarity as uniformly beneficial or uniformly harmful.

The learned crossing is not a human placement threshold. The hierarchical learner was already below the response target at the lower dense-grid boundary, so no corresponding hierarchical crossing was estimable. The numerical value therefore depends on how context and behavior are represented, as well as on the frozen payoff scale, learner parameters, context structure, and horizon. The bootstrap interval quantifies Monte Carlo uncertainty across the five simulated master-seed curves; it does not capture model-specification uncertainty or calibrate \(bf\) to academic self-concept. Those are empirical and sensitivity questions that remain open.

## Endogenous receptivity turns avoidance into a reciprocal process

M4 extends the cascade from learning about an environment to participating in its production. When masking reduced future receptivity, increasing \(k_m\) from 0 to 0.003 raised final-window masking by 0.409, from 0.304 to 0.712. Masking more than doubled because it altered the future alternative against which it was evaluated. The safe action did not merely prevent new information; it made later authenticity less likely to succeed.

This feedback offers a plausible mechanism for persistence beyond individual belief. Reduced visibility may limit opportunities for peers to learn a student's interests, for compatible relationships to form, or for teachers to create appropriate participation opportunities. A student may then encounter fewer positive responses, confirming the learned value of continued masking. The model captures the abstract structure of this loop without claiming that any one interpersonal pathway has been empirically established.

The continuation ramp added evidence of behavioral history. Across matched \(k_m\) values, descending and ascending paths differed by 0.065 in masking and -0.106 in receptivity. The same current parameter produced different outcomes because the earlier path had already altered both Q-values and the environment. This is schedule-specific path dependence.

It is not yet robust hysteresis. A stronger claim requires replicated ramp seeds, multiple ramp speeds, transition-threshold estimates, loop-area comparisons, and stochastic controls. The current model also uses a single scalar receptivity variable and two Q-values; it does not represent separate peers, network change, strategic reciprocity, or explicit learner beliefs about social feedback. The result identifies a mechanism worth testing, not a complete theory of classroom dynamics.

## Contribution to educational psychology

The model family contributes a sequential account to a literature that has mainly described perceptions and strategies. The stigma-of-giftedness paradigm explains why visibility can matter. Social-coping research identifies the actions students use to regulate it. Contextual studies show that acceptance and self-concept vary across peer groups. The present models add an account of how these elements can become temporally organized: adverse outcomes change learned value, learned value changes sampling, sampling determines evidence, and behavior may alter future opportunity.

This account preserves agency without individualizing blame. Masking is an action selected in response to experienced contingencies, not a defect located wholly inside the student. At the same time, calling it adaptive cannot end the analysis. A response can be sensible when learned and later become poorly matched to current conditions. Educational psychology therefore needs to ask both why the behavior was acquired and whether the evidence available to the learner remains representative of the environment.

The cascade also shows why superficially similar interventions target different mechanisms. Increasing reward for authenticity addresses payoff. Reducing the cost of a failed attempt addresses exposure. Creating a fresh-start context addresses persistent state. Signaling receptive groups addresses search. Reducing comparison pressure addresses context value. Repairing the social consequences of prolonged invisibility addresses feedback. Combining these indiscriminately would obscure which causal problem is being solved.

## Educational hypotheses and empirical predictions

The simulations justify testable hypotheses rather than direct intervention recommendations. Five predictions follow from the cascade.

First, a salient negative response to visible participation should reduce subsequent participation most strongly when later positive evidence is sparse. This is the central hot-stove interaction: the effect of rejection should depend not only on its severity but on the learner's subsequent access to corrective experience.

Second, lower-exposure participation should preserve sampling after a negative event. If graded disclosure operates as modeled, students offered credible low-stakes routes should continue testing the environment more than students facing an all-or-nothing visibility choice. The critical outcome is not immediate comfort alone but later information gathering and movement toward personally desired levels of participation.

Third, fresh starts should differ from simple reward improvement. M2 predicts that changing current incentives without resetting a stigmatized state may have less effect than movement into a context where prior social information no longer governs peer response. Conversely, if learned avoidance transfers across contexts, environmental reset alone may be insufficient.

Fourth, clear information about receptive settings should reduce the context-count effect. M3 predicts that additional groups are beneficial when their receptivity is legible and costly when it must be discovered through repeated context-specific failures. Trusted guides, explicit norms, and transferable safety signals should matter more as the number of alternatives grows.

Fifth, identical current conditions may produce different behavior after different histories. M4 predicts that prior masking and peer response will explain later participation beyond contemporaneous measures of classroom climate. A credible test therefore requires sequence data rather than a single cross-sectional measure of belonging or authenticity.

Repeated experience-sampling or micro-longitudinal classroom designs are well suited to these predictions. They could record the visibility and stakes of a contribution, perceived and observed peer response, the next opportunity to participate, the action selected, and movement between contexts. Preregistered operational definitions should distinguish concealment of ability from privacy, strategic self-presentation, and ordinary variability in participation. Non-gifted comparison groups would help determine which processes are specific to the social meaning of giftedness and which reflect general learning under social risk.

## Boundary conditions and limitations

The primary limitation is external validity. No human data were fitted or predicted. “Masking,” “authenticity,” “stigma,” “receptivity,” and “comparison cost” are operational labels for model components. Their measurement, developmental meaning, and equivalence across cultures remain untested. The simulations demonstrate what specified mechanisms can produce; they do not estimate how frequently those mechanisms operate in gifted students.

The statistical evidence is also bounded. Principal contrasts used moderate synthetic populations and one deterministic condition seed. Bootstrap intervals describe dispersion among learners within maintained runs, not uncertainty across independent master seeds or a human population. The 63.3% M2 result is the proportion of cells in a chosen 21 by 17 parameter grid, not a prevalence estimate. It may change with grid bounds, resolution, horizon, classification threshold, and random seed. Publication-grade claims about regime boundaries require independent-seed replication and threshold-local uncertainty.

The learning assumptions are deliberately simple. Constant \(\alpha\) and \(\tau\) preserve ongoing learning and exploration, so final-window masking is persistent over the observed horizon rather than mathematically absorbing. Learners do not use confidence bounds, counterfactual inference, episodic memory, social observation, or model-based planning. Peer outcomes are simplified, and the learner parameters have not been calibrated to temperament or stable individual differences.

Each extension also has specific limitations. M1 assigns independent values to disclosure levels and lacks generalization or a maintained direct binary-action ablation. M2 requires broader joint sensitivity across turnover, discounting, stigma payoffs, learning, exploration, initialization, and horizon. M3 fixes one good-context identity and omits multiple receptive contexts. Its comparison cost is imposed rather than empirically estimated. M4 uses one ramp schedule and one seed sequence, and its scalar receptivity compresses a complex social network into a single evolving probability.

Finally, the models are a conceptual cascade, not a jointly estimated developmental system. M3 does not simultaneously contain M2's stigma states, and M4 does not combine endogenous receptivity with multiple peer contexts. This isolation supports mechanism identification but limits claims about interaction among mechanisms. A larger omnibus model should not be the immediate solution: additional complexity would be useful only after data establish which component interactions are empirically necessary.

## Conclusion

The study demonstrates that persistent masking need not be either a fixed personal preference or a rational response to an objectively unfavorable environment. Under the tested learning rule, authenticity was dynamically optimal across large regions in which masking nevertheless dominated behavior. Hot-stove information restriction supplied the core mechanism: negative experience reduced later sampling and restricted the evidence needed for correction.

The remaining models show how the surrounding decision structure changes that process. Negative weighting and sticky stigma deepen avoidance. Graded disclosure preserves lower-exposure contact with information. Multidimensional search makes receptive contexts difficult to discover when safety is easy and authenticity is context-specific. Comparison costs can offset the value of acceptance. Endogenous receptivity allows present masking to reduce future opportunity and retain behavioral history.

The broad educational implication is not that authenticity should be demanded. It is that persistent low visibility cannot be interpreted solely from current behavior or current climate. Understanding it requires the learner's sequence of outcomes, the alternatives that remained available, the legibility of receptive contexts, and the ways prior behavior altered later opportunity. The model cascade turns those considerations into distinct, falsifiable hypotheses. Human longitudinal evidence must now determine which mechanisms operate, for whom, and under what educational conditions.
