# The Model Cascade: From Risky Choice to Endogenous Social Opportunity

The five models form a controlled conceptual cascade. M0 begins with the smallest learning problem capable of producing persistent masking: a repeated choice between a safe action and a risky authentic action. M1 asks whether that result changes when authenticity can be expressed gradually rather than all at once. M2 adds environmental memory, so an authentic outcome can change the learner's future state and not merely the reward received on the current trial. M3 adds a second dimension of choice by requiring the learner to identify a receptive social context as well as decide whether to mask within it. M4 closes the causal loop by allowing behavior to alter later receptivity. Across the cascade, the problem therefore expands from risky choice, to action granularity, to state persistence, to contextual search, and finally to reciprocal behavior-environment feedback.

This ordering is logical rather than developmental. The models are not proposed stages through which gifted children necessarily progress, and they are not five fitted descriptions of one human population. Nor are they strictly nested pieces of one increasingly elaborate computer model. Each retains the shared reinforcement-learning core while introducing a deliberately limited structural change. M2 returns to two actions while adding state; M3 removes the stigma-state process while adding context search; M4 again uses two action values while making receptivity endogenous. This selective construction is essential to the research design: it allows each candidate mechanism to be tested without silently attributing an effect to several new assumptions at once.

| Model | Starting problem | New element | Question made testable |
|---|---|---|---|
| M0 | Repeated mask-or-authenticate choice | Risky authentic outcomes | Can learned masking persist when authenticity has higher expected value? |
| M1 | Risky authenticity | Five disclosure levels | Can lower-exposure actions preserve exploration? |
| M2 | Two-action learning | Neutral, accepted, and stigmatized states | How do lasting consequences alter optimal and learned policies? |
| M3 | Choice between masking and authenticity | Multiple contexts and comparison cost | Does search burden obstruct discovery of a receptive setting? |
| M4 | Two-action bandit with fixed receptivity | Behavior-dependent receptivity | Can masking reduce its own future alternatives and create path dependence? |

## The shared learner: experience updates value and value controls experience

All five models use the same basic learning logic. Each learner begins with action values initialized to \(q_0\) and updates the value of the chosen action using learning rate \(\alpha\). In the bandit models, the update is

\[
Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a)+\alpha[v(R)-Q(a)],
\]

where \(R\) is the objective reward generated by the environment and \(v(R)\) is the reward as processed by the learner. M2 uses the corresponding Q-learning target,

\[
v(R)+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a'),
\]

so the learned value of an action includes the discounted value of the next state.

The subjective transform separates environmental value from sensitivity to negative experience. Nonnegative rewards are learned at their objective value, \(v(R)=R\). Negative rewards are multiplied by \(\lambda\), so \(v(R)=\lambda R\) when \(R<0\). Increasing \(\lambda\) therefore makes an adverse experience more influential in learning without changing the payoff produced by the environment. This separation is what permits the models to distinguish a harsh environment from a learner who updates more strongly after the same negative event.

Actions are selected with a temperature-controlled softmax policy. Higher-valued actions are chosen more often, but exploration continues according to temperature \(\tau\). This produces the cascade's central feedback even before the environment itself becomes dynamic: experience changes Q-values, Q-values change what is sampled, and altered sampling changes the evidence available for later learning. A rejected authentic action can therefore become a “hot stove.” Its estimated value falls, it is selected less often, and the learner receives fewer opportunities to observe that it can also succeed.

Unless varied deliberately, the maintained simulations used \(\alpha=0.10\), \(\tau=0.25\), \(q_0=0\), and \(\lambda=1\). Every parameter cell used a recorded local NumPy random generator rather than global random state. Primary behavioral outcomes were calculated over the final 20% of each run. These common choices make differences across models interpretable as consequences of the changed decision structure rather than unannounced changes to the learner.

## M0: risky authenticity establishes the minimal masking problem

M0 contains only two actions: masking and authenticity. Masking produces the certain reward

\[
R_{mask}=b-c,
\]

where \(b\) is the social benefit of belonging and \(c\) is the ongoing cost of concealment. Authenticity is accepted with probability \(p\). Acceptance produces \(b+a\), combining belonging with the intrinsic value \(a\) of authentic expression. Rejection produces \(a-r\), where \(r\) is the rejection penalty. Its expected objective reward is therefore

\[
E(auth)=p(b+a)+(1-p)(a-r)=a+pb-(1-p)r.
\]

With the default values \(b=1.0\), \(a=0.6\), \(c=0.25\), and \(r=2.0\), masking is worth 0.75 and authenticity is worth \(-1.4+3p\). Authenticity becomes objectively preferable at \(p=0.7167\). This analytic threshold is central to the entire cascade. It marks the point above which persistent masking cannot be explained simply by saying that masking pays more.

M0 isolates a pure information problem. The environment has no memory, the learner has no explicit psychological state, and there is no need to choose among peer contexts. If masking persists where \(p>0.7167\), the cause must lie in the interaction between stochastic experience and the learner's sampling rule. An early rejection depresses the learned value of authenticity; the safe action then receives more trials; and the learner may fail to collect enough later acceptances to correct its estimate. M0 thus supplies the minimal hot-stove mechanism on which the rest of the cascade builds.

The maintained domain tested receptivity directly at \(p=0.20\) and \(p=0.85\), using 250 learners per condition for 900 steps. It separately tested negative-outcome weighting at \(p=0.65\), comparing \(\lambda=0.50\) and \(\lambda=2.50\) with the same population and horizon. Analytic checks covered the endpoint cases \(p=0\) and \(p=1\), the indifference threshold, the separation of objective and subjective reward, and numerical stability of action selection.

What M0 does not contain is equally important. Masking and authenticity are binary; acceptance or rejection has no lasting environmental consequence; the social context is known and fixed; and the learner cannot change receptivity. M0 answers whether risky sampling alone can sustain masking. It cannot answer whether partial participation, reputational states, contextual search, or reciprocal social feedback alter that result.

## M1: graded disclosure adds action granularity

M1 changes the action space while preserving the stationary bandit environment. Instead of choosing only masking or full authenticity, the learner selects one of five evenly spaced disclosure levels \(d\in\{0,0.25,0.50,0.75,1\}\). The endpoints preserve the M0 meanings: \(d=0\) is full masking and \(d=1\) is full authenticity. Acceptance probability declines linearly with exposure,

\[
Pr(accept\mid d)=1-(1-p)d,
\]

and expected objective reward is

\[
E(d)=b-c+d[a+c-(1-p)(b+r)].
\]

The function is linear in disclosure. Consequently, intermediate disclosure is not generally a new static optimum; except at indifference, one endpoint has the greatest expected reward. What M1 adds is not a claim that partial authenticity is inherently best. It adds lower-exposure experiments. A learner can sample some visibility while accepting less rejection risk than full disclosure would impose.

This rung changes the exploration geometry. In M0, testing authenticity requires assuming its entire risk at once. In M1, the learner may continue to encounter informative social outcomes through intermediate actions after full disclosure has become unattractive. The model therefore asks whether the available action vocabulary influences what can be learned. It represents a distinction between final policy value and exploratory value: an action can be useful because it preserves contact with evidence even when it is not the best long-run endpoint.

The maintained M1 contrast tested \(p=0.20\) against \(p=0.85\), with five disclosure arms, 250 learners per condition, and 900 steps. Mean disclosure over the final 20% of choices was the principal outcome. Each disclosure level had an independent Q-value. The model therefore tests whether lower-exposure alternatives change sampling, but it does not implement smooth generalization between neighboring levels. Success at \(d=0.50\), for example, does not automatically raise the learned value of \(d=0.75\). A stronger model of developmental “ladder climbing” would need structured or continuous representations that transfer information across disclosure levels, as well as a direct maintained comparison with an otherwise identical binary action space.

## M2: stigma states add environmental memory and long-run value

M2 returns to the two M0 actions but makes their consequences state-dependent. The learner can occupy one of three explicit states: neutral, accepted-as-self, or stigmatized. Q-values are stored separately for each state and action. This is the first rung in which the value of authenticity depends not only on its immediate reward but also on the state it opens for subsequent decisions.

In the neutral state, masking returns \(b-c\) and leaves the learner neutral. Authentic acceptance occurs with probability \(p\), returns \(b+a\), and moves the learner to the accepted state. Authentic rejection returns \(a-r\) and moves the learner to the stigmatized state. In the accepted state, authenticity reliably returns \(b+a\), whereas masking returns \(b-c\). Independent peer turnover with probability \(\delta\) can then reset the next state to neutral.

The stigmatized state makes adverse history durable. Masking returns \(b_2-c\) and does not automatically erase stigma. Authenticity redeems the learner with probability \(p/2\), returning \(b+a\) and moving to accepted; failure returns \(a-r_2\). If the next state remains stigmatized, escape with probability \(\eta\) resets it to neutral. Rewards are generated from the current state before turnover or escape acts on the next state. This ordering means that recovery changes future opportunity but does not retroactively alter the current outcome.

M2 adds two distinct possibilities to the cascade. First, acceptance can make authenticity dynamically valuable even when its one-step expected value is unfavorable, because it opens a reliably rewarding state. Second, rejection can impose continuing costs, so masking may become optimal under sufficiently harsh transition structures. These possibilities cannot be distinguished by mask rate alone. The model therefore pairs Q-learning with value iteration: the learned neutral-state action is compared with the optimal policy of the same objective-reward Markov decision process. This separates learned suboptimal masking from genuine structural conditions in which an optimal planner should also mask.

The default M2 environment used \(b_2=0.5\), \(r_2=2.5\), \(\delta=0.02\), and \(\gamma=0.9\). The principal contrast fixed \(p=0.55\) and compared \(\eta=0\) with \(\eta=0.20\), using 200 learners per cell for 1,200 steps. The broader regime map tested a 21 by 17 grid: \(p\) ranged from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.05, and \(\eta\) ranged from 0 to 0.80 in increments of 0.05. Each cell used 160 learners for 1,000 steps and a recorded cell-specific seed. Refined value-iteration thresholds and learner-parameter sensitivity analyses supplemented the coarse map.

M2 is the cascade's decisive benchmark for the central claim because it can say whether authenticity is optimal over time, not merely whether it has the larger immediate expectation. Its limits are also specific. The states are imposed abstractions rather than observed psychological categories; transition probabilities are not fitted to human data; and the default map does not exhaust the joint domain of turnover, discounting, stigmatized rewards, learning rate, exploration, initialization, and horizon.

## M3: context search adds a second decision dimension

M3 shifts the problem from learning what to do in one environment to discovering where authenticity is viable. There are \(K\) peer contexts. One “good” context has receptivity \(p_{good}=0.85\), while the remaining contexts have \(p_{bad}=0.20\). In the original flat formulation, every context supplies a masking arm and an authenticity arm, producing \(2K\) actions. Masking returns \(b-c\) everywhere. Authenticity uses the receptivity of the selected context.

This structure adds an asymmetric search problem. Safe masking is reproducible across contexts, but the high-value authentic option is concentrated in a rare location. As \(K\) grows, the learner must allocate experience across more context-behavior combinations to identify the receptive one. A low rate of good-context authenticity can therefore arise even though the good context itself has not become less receptive.

M3 also introduces a comparison cost \(bf\), subtracted only from accepted authenticity in the good context. This represents the possibility that a welcoming high-performing setting still carries a self-evaluative cost. Under the default payoffs, masking has expected reward \(0.75\), good-context authenticity has expected reward \(1.15-0.85bf\), and their exact objective crossover is \(bf=8/17=0.470588\). The comparison mechanism is distinct from rejection: it can offset the benefit of acceptance inside the most receptive context.

The key methodological addition is not merely a larger \(K\), but an ablation of action representation. Four formulations were compared: the flat \(2K\)-arm model; a hierarchical model that first chose context and then behavior; a global-mask model with one shared masking action and \(K\) context-specific authenticity actions; and a fixed-good-context model that removed context search. Comparing these variants tests whether the result belongs to the social environment or to the way the decision problem is represented.

The completed context-search experiments tested K-response curves and action-space controls across five independent master seeds, including randomized good-context identity and multiple-good-context variants. E05 then isolated comparison cost at \(K=4\) with fixed good-context identity. It tested 18 prescribed \(bf\) values in both flat-2K and hierarchical representations, using 500 learners per value and master seed for 2,000 steps. Settling was defined by the modal action during the final 20% of trials. A prespecified nonincreasing PAVA fit located an architecture-specific 0.5 response crossing, and a 10,000-resample master-seed bootstrap quantified its uncertainty. The analytic objective crossover was always reported separately from this learned transition.

This design makes E05 a direct comparison between what the payoff equation favors and what the learner comes to select. In flat 2K, the learned crossing occurred at \(bf=0.312852\), before the exact objective crossover at \(0.470588\). In the hierarchical representation, the response was already below 0.5 at the dense-range lower boundary, so no crossing was estimable. The model therefore tests a learned-objective separation in one frozen representation, not a universal comparison-cost threshold.

## M4: endogenous receptivity closes the feedback loop

M4 makes the largest conceptual change while returning to two action values. In M0 through M3, receptivity is supplied by the environment. In M4, each learner has a current receptivity \(p_t\) that evolves within fixed bounds \(p_{min}=0.05\) and \(p_{max}=0.95\). Rewards are sampled from the current \(p_t\), the selected Q-value is updated, and receptivity then changes according to the outcome:

\[
p_{t+1}=p_t+k_a(p_{max}-p_t)
\]

after accepted authenticity,

\[
p_{t+1}=p_t-k_r(p_t-p_{min})
\]

after rejected authenticity, and

\[
p_{t+1}=p_t-k_m(p_t-p_{min})
\]

after masking. Every update is clipped to the stated bounds.

M4 closes a feedback loop that the preceding models leave open. In M0, behavior changes what the learner knows but not what the environment offers. In M2, behavior changes the learner's categorical state according to fixed transitions. In M4, behavior changes the probability of later acceptance itself. Authentic success can make future authenticity safer; rejection can make it less safe; and masking can gradually erode receptivity through reduced visibility, weakened relationship formation, or diminished opportunity for peers to respond positively. Masking is therefore capable of protecting the learner in the present while degrading the future alternative against which it is evaluated.

The model retained only two Q-values and did not condition them directly on \(p_t\). It is therefore an endogenous nonstationary bandit, not a stateful Q-learning model. The learner does not explicitly represent the evolving environment or plan over its feedback dynamics. This limitation is theoretically useful: any path dependence arises from ordinary value learning interacting with a changing opportunity structure, not from a sophisticated learner anticipating long-run social consequences.

The primary M4 contrast used \(p_0=0.45\), \(k_a=0.12\), \(k_r=0.04\), and compared \(k_m=0\) with \(k_m=0.003\). Each cell contained 220 learners and ran for 1,200 steps. The path experiment increased \(k_m\) over seven evenly spaced values from 0 to 0.003 and then decreased it over the same grid. At every ramp point, 220 learners continued from their current Q-values and \(p_t\) for another 500 steps. Because the descending path inherited the endpoint of the ascending path, the experiment tested whether identical current parameters produced different outcomes after different histories.

The tested domain supports a claim of feedback and schedule-specific path dependence. It does not yet support general hysteresis or irreversible entrenchment. The ramp used one speed and one seed sequence, and it lacked stochastic-control loop areas and formal transition-threshold comparisons. M4 also represents receptivity with one scalar shared across the learner's social environment; it does not model distinct peers, network turnover, strategic response, or explicit inference about how behavior affects others.

## What the cascade establishes—and what it deliberately leaves unresolved

Taken together, the models decompose persistent masking into progressively richer problems. M0 shows how stochastic punishment can distort later evidence even in a stationary two-action world. M1 shows that the shape of the action space determines whether lower-risk exploration remains available. M2 shows that current outcomes can open or close future states and supplies an optimal-policy benchmark for distinguishing learner failure from rational adaptation. M3 shows that a receptive opportunity may have little practical value when it is difficult to locate among context-specific alternatives. M4 shows how learned behavior and social opportunity can become mutually reinforcing over time.

The cascade's strength is identification through simplicity. Each model is transparent enough for its reward equations, thresholds, transition order, action indexing, or feedback rule to be tested directly. The price of that transparency is abstraction. The learners do not perceive ambiguous intentions, infer other people's beliefs, generalize flexibly across similar settings, or choose among the multiple motives that human students may have for selective disclosure. “Masking” denotes the safer and less visibly authentic action under the specified payoff structure; it is not assumed to represent every form of privacy, impression management, code-switching, or concealment.

The tested domain is consequently a mechanism-exposure domain rather than an estimated human parameter domain. Parameters were chosen to reveal thresholds, edge cases, and causal distinctions, not fitted to observations of gifted children. Most principal contrasts used 200 to 250 synthetic learners and 900 to 1,200 steps, with outcomes summarized over the last fifth of behavior. The broader M2 grid used 160 learners per cell and one recorded seed per cell. The M4 ramp continued the same simulated histories by design. These choices are appropriate for establishing what the specified models can produce, but they do not yield population prevalence, developmental timing, clinical classification, or intervention effect sizes.

The models should therefore be read as a sequence of propositions. Risk can restrict information; action granularity can preserve sampling; state transitions can make consequences durable; context structure can burden discovery; and behavior can change future opportunity. The cascade shows how these mechanisms fit together conceptually without pretending that they have already been jointly estimated in human development. Its next scientific step is not to add every mechanism to one maximal simulation, but to test the distinctive predictions of each rung empirically and to determine which combinations are necessary to explain observed sequences of participation, peer response, context change, and masking.
