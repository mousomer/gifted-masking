# Results by Finding: When Optimal Authenticity Remains Unlearned

The simulations produced a clear central result: risky authenticity can generate persistent learned masking even when authenticity is the objectively better policy. This was not a marginal anomaly confined to an indifference boundary. In the maintained M2 phase grid, learned suboptimal masking occupied 226 of 357 tested cells (63.3%), and the mean learned probability of masking in those cells was 0.950. The model ladder then identified mechanisms that enlarged, reduced, or redirected this regime. Negative outcomes discouraged later sampling; heavier weighting of those outcomes amplified avoidance; sticky stigma prolonged their consequences; graded disclosure provided lower-exposure routes for continued exploration; multidimensional context search made receptive settings harder to discover; sufficiently large comparison costs eliminated the advantage of an otherwise welcoming context; and behavior-dependent receptivity made masking self-reinforcing and path-dependent. The analytic results establish where authenticity was objectively preferable. The simulations establish that the tested learners frequently failed to discover or maintain that policy. They do not establish the prevalence of these mechanisms in human students.

## Objective benchmarks made suboptimal masking identifiable

The first result was mathematical rather than stochastic. Under the default M0 rewards, masking had expected value \(b-c=0.75\), whereas authenticity had expected value \(a+pb-(1-p)r=-1.4+3p\). Equating the two gave an objective authenticity threshold of \(p=0.7167\). Above this threshold, authenticity was the higher-value action regardless of what the learner ultimately selected. This benchmark converted a high observed mask rate from a descriptive pattern into a testable learning failure: masking above the threshold was suboptimal under the model's own reward function.

M1 reproduced the M0 endpoints exactly. Its expected reward was linear in disclosure, so intermediate disclosure levels did not create a new static optimum. Their potential value was procedural: they changed the exposure and trajectory of exploration. M3 supplied a second analytic benchmark. In the receptive context, the comparison-cost crossover was \(bf=0.4706\); below it, good-context authenticity was objectively preferable to masking, and above it, the imposed comparison cost removed that advantage.

The M2 benchmark was stronger because it evaluated long-run state-contingent value rather than one-step expected reward. Value iteration showed that, under the default transition structure, authenticity became optimal in the neutral state at receptivity levels well below the static M0 threshold: \(p=0.3839\) when stigma escape \(\eta=0\), \(p=0.3388\) when \(\eta=0.20\), and \(p=0.2690\) when \(\eta=0.80\). Acceptance was dynamically valuable because it opened an accepted state with reliably favorable subsequent returns. These thresholds prove which policy maximized expected discounted value in the specified MDP. They do not prove that a reinforcement learner with limited sampling will discover that policy.

## Risky authenticity produced persistent learned masking

M0 established the basic behavioral effect. At low receptivity, \(p=0.20\), the mean final-window mask rate was 0.995. At high receptivity, \(p=0.85\), it was 0.262. The absolute difference was 0.733, with a 95% independent-bootstrap interval from 0.721 to 0.745 and 250 learners per condition. Put comparatively, raising receptivity across these conditions reduced masking by 73.7% relative to its near-ceiling low-receptivity value. The effect was therefore both statistically precise within the simulated samples and substantively large: the dominant behavior changed from almost universal masking to masking in roughly one quarter of final-window choices.

This contrast alone does not demonstrate suboptimal masking at \(p=0.20\), because masking is objectively advantageous in that condition. Its importance is mechanistic: it verifies that risky authenticity is sufficient to produce sharply different learned policies. The objective benchmark then allows the stronger claim to be tested in M2, where learned behavior can be compared directly with the optimal dynamic policy.

Negative-outcome weighting strongly magnified avoidance without changing environmental value. At \(p=0.65\), increasing \(\lambda\) from 0.50 to 2.50 raised the final-window mask rate from 0.586 to 0.990. The absolute increase was 0.403 (95% interval 0.386 to 0.421; 250 learners per condition), equivalent to a 68.8% increase relative to the lower-weighting condition. Because \(\lambda\) transformed negative learning signals but did not alter objective rewards, this effect isolates sensitivity to adverse experience within the learner. It does not show that real students possess a corresponding stable trait, nor does it calibrate \(\lambda\) to temperament, diagnosis, or measured loss aversion.

## Hot-stove learning generated a large suboptimal-masking regime

The strongest evidence came from M2. At \(p=0.55\), value iteration selected authenticity as the optimal neutral-state action both when stigma could not be escaped and when the escape probability was \(\eta=0.20\). The learners nevertheless assigned masking neutral-state choice probabilities of 0.984 and 0.973. Their corresponding neutral-state objective regrets were 3.472 and 3.575. Thus, the discrepancy was not a comparison between learned behavior and an informal expectation that authenticity “should” be better; it was a measured gap between the learned policy and the model's computed optimum.

Sticky stigma increased observed masking, but it was not the sole cause of the failure. Raising \(\eta\) from 0 to 0.20 reduced the mean final-window mask rate from 0.785 to 0.584, an absolute reduction of 0.201 (95% interval 0.115 to 0.287; 200 learners per condition), or 25.6% relative to the no-escape condition. Escape therefore had a meaningful protective effect. Yet the learned neutral-state mask probability remained above 0.97 in both cells. Stigma persistence deepened the avoidance regime, while the preference for the safe action already existed because adverse authentic outcomes reduced later sampling.

The phase-grid result showed that this failure was broad. Across the maintained 21 by 17 grid of receptivity and stigma escape, 226 of 357 cells (63.3%) met the classification for learned suboptimal masking. Within those cells, the mean neutral-state masking probability was 0.950. Classified cells extended from \(p=0.40\) to 0.95 at low escape rates and from \(p=0.30\) to 0.95 when \(\eta\geq0.40\). The result therefore covered substantial areas in which receptivity was moderate or high, not merely cells close to objective indifference.

Exploration sensitivity supported the hot-stove interpretation. At \(p=0.75\) and \(\eta=0\), reducing the exploration temperature increased neutral-state masking from the default value of 0.950 to 0.994, whereas higher exploration reduced it to 0.649. Relative to the default, stronger exploration removed 0.301 of masking probability, a 31.7% reduction, although masking remained common. The direction of this result is central: when the learner sampled the devalued authentic action more often, it gained greater access to corrective positive evidence. When sampling contracted, early adverse evidence became more persistent.

The default M2 result is therefore learned suboptimal masking, not a structural trap in which masking is the optimal dynamic policy. A separate sweep did identify genuine optimal-policy masking above the static M0 threshold, but only under harsher tested settings. Its largest observed width was 0.0563 when the stigmatized rejection penalty was 8, turnover was 0.30, escape was zero, and \(\gamma=0.9\). Structural lock-in is possible in the model, but it is narrow and parameter-contingent. The much larger default regime arises because the learner fails to discover or sustain the dynamically optimal action.

## Graded disclosure created a lower-exposure route for exploration

M1 showed that disclosure need not be represented as an all-or-nothing action. Mean final-window disclosure was 0.048 at \(p=0.20\) and 0.617 at \(p=0.85\), an absolute increase of 0.569 (95% interval 0.556 to 0.582; 250 learners per condition). The high-receptivity mean was nearly 13 times the low-receptivity mean, although the absolute difference is the more stable measure because the low condition was close to zero.

The analytic and simulated results together clarify what this finding means. Intermediate disclosure was not generally a superior endpoint: the expected-reward function was linear, and each disclosure level had an independent learned value. Instead, intermediate actions reduced exposure to rejection during sampling. They allowed the learner to remain in contact with informative outcomes at levels of visibility below full authenticity. In that sense, graded disclosure supplied an exploratory bridge rather than a new optimum.

The maintained contrast directly establishes that receptivity strongly changes learned disclosure within the graded model. The stronger claim that a graded action space outperforms an otherwise identical binary learner requires a maintained binary-versus-graded ablation. The mechanism is present in the action design, but its incremental behavioral benefit should be quantified directly before assigning it a precise intervention effect.

## Multidimensional search enlarged the burden of finding authenticity

M3 demonstrated that a receptive context can exist without being reliably discovered. In the original flat 2K-action representation, the proportion of learners settling on authenticity in the good context declined from 0.964 at \(K=1\) to 0.764 at \(K=8\). The absolute gap was 0.200 (95% interval 0.141 to 0.264; 220 learners per condition), a 20.7% reduction relative to the single-context condition.

The action-space ablations identified the active mechanism. The \(K=1\) minus \(K=8\) gap was 0.168 in the flat representation and 0.377 in the hierarchical context-then-behavior representation. It collapsed to 0.005 when masking was represented as one global action and to -0.005 when the good context was fixed and known. The largest effect, in the hierarchical representation, was more than twice the flat-model gap. Conversely, removing the need to discover the receptive context eliminated essentially the entire effect.

These comparisons rule out the generic conclusion that more contexts inherently produce more masking. Context count mattered when it expanded a multidimensional search problem in which a rare receptive alternative had to be identified while a safe masking response remained widely available. The relevant environmental property is therefore legibility: how readily learners can identify, test, and remember which contexts are receptive. The result predicts that additional social options will help only when their receptivity can be recognized or explored efficiently.

## Learned retreat preceded the objective comparison-cost crossover

E05 paired an exact objective benchmark with a dense learned-response sweep. At the frozen payoffs, masking had expected reward \(0.75\), while authenticity in the receptive context had expected reward \(1.15-0.85bf\). Its objective advantage was therefore \(0.40-0.85bf\), giving the exact objective crossover \(bf=8/17=0.470588\). Below that value receptive-context authenticity remained objectively preferable to masking; above it, the imposed comparison cost reversed the ordering.

The corresponding flat-2K simulation showed that learned behavior retreated considerably earlier. Across 18 comparison-cost values, five independent master seeds, and 500 learners per seed and value followed for 2,000 trials, the fraction whose modal final-window action was receptive-context authenticity crossed 0.5 at \(bf=0.312852\). The architecture-isolated master-seed bootstrap interval was \(0.307836\) to \(0.316918\); every seed-level curve crossed inside the prespecified range, all 10,000 bootstrap resamples yielded valid crossings, and no adjacent dense-grid contrast had a material upward violation.

The separation between thresholds was substantive. The learned crossing preceded objective indifference by \(0.157736\). At that learned crossing, authenticity still retained an objective advantage of approximately \(0.134\), roughly one-third of its initial \(0.40\) advantage. Thus E05 did more than show that a sufficiently large cost can make authenticity objectively unattractive: in the frozen flat representation, most learners stopped settling on receptive-context authenticity while it was still the higher-value static action.

The full response was large. The good-context-authentic modal fraction fell from 0.940 at \(bf=0\) to zero at \(bf=0.80\), an endpoint change of \(-0.940\) (95% five-seed Student-t interval \(-0.958\) to \(-0.922\)). At the exact objective crossover, only 0.142 of learners had receptive-context authenticity as their modal action, while the mean final-window masking rate was 0.852.

The numerical transition did not generalize across action representations. In the hierarchical context-then-behavior learner, the receptive-context-authentic modal fraction was already 0.118 at the lower dense-grid boundary \(bf=0.30\). None of its five seed curves, and none of 10,000 bootstrap curves, produced an in-range crossing. This is a censored boundary result rather than evidence that no hierarchical transition exists. It prevents the flat \(bf=0.313\) crossing from being presented as an architecture-general or human threshold.

E05 establishes a learned-objective gap, not its unique cause. The result is consistent with risky exploration, hot-stove persistence, and multidimensional action competition, but the experiment did not remove those mechanisms one at a time. Moreover, \(bf\) is an imposed payoff term rather than an empirically calibrated measure of academic self-concept, and the transition remains conditional on the frozen learner, context structure, and 2,000-trial horizon.

## Endogenous receptivity made masking self-reinforcing and path-dependent

M4 changed receptivity from a fixed environmental probability into an outcome partly produced by prior behavior. When masking reduced future receptivity, increasing the masking-feedback parameter \(k_m\) from 0 to 0.003 raised final-window masking from 0.304 to 0.712. The absolute increase was 0.409 (95% interval 0.338 to 0.478; 220 learners per condition). Masking therefore increased by approximately 134% relative to the no-feedback condition, or from roughly three in ten to more than seven in ten choices.

The continuation ramp showed that the effect retained behavioral history. Across seven matched values of \(k_m\), the mean descending-minus-ascending mask-rate gap was 0.065, while the corresponding receptivity gap was -0.106. The same current parameter value therefore produced different outcomes depending on whether it had been approached along the ascending or descending path. This occurred because the earlier path had already altered both learned action values and environmental receptivity.

The result establishes self-reinforcing feedback by construction and schedule-specific path dependence by simulation. It does not yet establish robust hysteresis. That stronger conclusion would require independent ramp seeds, multiple ramp speeds, transition-threshold separation, loop-area estimates, and stochastic controls showing that the path gap exceeds ordinary trajectory variation.

## Scope and limitations of the findings

The findings are strong within the tested models but deliberately bounded. First, analytic thresholds are proved consequences of the specified reward and transition equations; behavioral effects are Monte Carlo results under particular learner rules, parameters, horizons, and action representations. Neither kind of result demonstrates how prevalent masking mechanisms are among gifted students. “Authenticity,” “masking,” “stigma,” and “receptivity” are operational labels whose correspondence to human constructs remains to be established.

Second, the reported confidence intervals describe variation among synthetic learners within maintained runs. The principal contrasts used one deterministic seed per condition and moderate population sizes. These intervals do not replace replication across independent master seeds. Likewise, the finding that 63.3% of the M2 grid exhibited learned suboptimal masking describes coverage of the chosen 21 by 17 parameter grid. It is not a population prevalence estimate, and its exact value may change with grid bounds, resolution, horizon, classification threshold, and seed.

Third, the learners used constant learning and exploration parameters. High final-window masking therefore means persistent behavior over the observed horizon, not an absorbing or irreversible state. Results may differ under decaying learning rates, explicit uncertainty-directed exploration, memory, correlated peer outcomes, model-based planning, or longer horizons. A direct forced-exploration ablation would also strengthen the causal claim that information restriction is the critical link in hot-stove persistence.

Fourth, each extended model retains specific unresolved boundaries. M1 represents disclosure levels as independent arms and does not implement generalization between neighboring actions. M2 needs broader joint sensitivity across learning, discounting, turnover, stigma, initialization, horizon, and escape parameters. M3's dense comparison-cost result remains specific to the frozen context structure, learner, action representation, and horizon; its payoff term still requires empirical calibration before it can support claims about academic placement. M4 requires the additional ramp and stochastic controls needed to distinguish reproducible hysteresis from schedule-specific path dependence.

Within those boundaries, the conclusion is nevertheless substantive. Persistent masking was not confined to environments in which masking was objectively best. It dominated large portions of the maintained authenticity-optimal M2 grid because adverse experience reduced later sampling and access to corrective information. Negative-outcome weighting and sticky stigma deepened that avoidance; graded disclosure offered lower-exposure sampling routes; multidimensional search obstructed discovery of receptive contexts; comparison costs could erase their advantage; and endogenous feedback allowed present masking to worsen future opportunity. The models therefore identify a coherent family of mechanisms through which a learner can remain hidden from opportunities that are already worth taking.
