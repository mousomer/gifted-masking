# Literature Review: From Social Coping to Learned Persistent Masking

Research on gifted students presents an apparent contradiction. Population-level evidence does not support treating high cognitive ability as a general social or psychological deficit, yet a substantial qualitative and measurement literature shows that some gifted students actively manage how visible their ability is to others. They may deny giftedness, moderate performance, conform to peers, deflect attention, use humor, or make their ability socially useful through helping. These findings establish that reduced visibility is a real and context-sensitive form of social coping. They do not yet explain how an initially strategic response becomes persistent, why it can survive after conditions improve, or why a receptive context may remain undiscovered. The present study addresses that missing sequential mechanism.

The literature review therefore proceeds from a deliberately bounded premise. Masking is not assumed to be a defining characteristic of giftedness, evidence of psychopathology, or an irrational failure to “be authentic.” It is treated as one possible behavioral response when visible difference has uncertain social consequences. The central theoretical question is not whether gifted students are inherently risk averse. It is whether repeated experience can make a safe, low-visibility response persist even when visible authenticity has become the better available option.

## Giftedness does not imply a uniform social-emotional profile

Any account of masking must begin by rejecting the deficit stereotype it could otherwise reproduce. Gifted and high-ability students are heterogeneous in identification, cognitive profile, educational placement, culture, socioeconomic position, peer ecology, and developmental history. Reviews of non-cognitive characteristics do not identify a single personality or adjustment profile that separates all intellectually gifted young people from their peers. Tourreix et al. (2023) found inconsistent patterns across social, emotional, motivational, and personality domains and emphasized the need to distinguish empirically supported characteristics from recurring cultural myths.

Large community-sample evidence points in the same direction. Lavrijsen and Verschueren (2023) reported that adolescents with measured high cognitive ability showed similar or lower levels of several mental-health difficulties than average-ability peers. Formally identified gifted adolescents in the same study reported more difficulties on some outcomes, but that contrast cannot be read as a direct effect of cognitive ability. Identification, selection into services, labeling, family help-seeking, and pre-existing concerns may all contribute. The important conclusion is not that gifted students never experience difficulty; it is that difficulty cannot be presumed from giftedness alone.

This distinction changes the explanatory task. If most high-ability students are not uniformly socially impaired, then concealment should not be modeled as the automatic expression of a gifted personality. It is more plausibly investigated as a conditional response arising in particular social environments. The relevant unit of explanation becomes the interaction between a learner, a visible behavior, and a history of peer consequences.

Contextual evidence reinforces that position. Verschueren et al. (2019) studied 2,736 sixth-grade students, including 274 high-ability students, and found that high-ability youth were better accepted than average-ability peers according to teachers and classmates. Their self-perceptions were not correspondingly more positive, however, and they nominated fewer peers as friends. High-ability students also felt more accepted in classes with higher mean ability. These differences across observers and classrooms are incompatible with a simple claim that high ability either guarantees or prevents social integration. They point instead to person-group fit, local norms, and perceived rather than merely externally rated acceptance.

## The stigma-of-giftedness literature identifies visibility as a social problem

The stigma-of-giftedness tradition supplies the most direct foundation for the present models. Coleman and Cross (1988) asked whether giftedness could operate as a social handicap. Their interviews supported a qualified conclusion: many, but not all, gifted adolescents perceived their difference as interfering with ordinary social acceptance. The value of this work lies in its conditional formulation. Giftedness was not defined as inherently stigmatizing; rather, its visibility could become consequential in settings where peers interpreted exceptional ability as socially deviant, threatening, or incompatible with group membership.

Cross et al. (1993) formalized this account around three propositions. Gifted students generally desire ordinary social interaction; they may expect others to treat them differently when giftedness becomes known; and they can influence those interactions by managing the information available to others. In a questionnaire study of 1,465 adolescents attending governor's school programs, the stigma-of-giftedness paradigm provided a coherent framework for understanding school-based social cognition. The key move was to treat students as strategic social actors. They were not merely recipients of labeling; they could alter the visibility, framing, and timing of information about themselves.

Later scenario-based work made this management process more concrete. Cross et al. (2014) described a continuum of visibility ranging from direct truthful expression through placating, deflection, cover-up, and explicit denial. Responses varied across potentially stigmatizing school situations. This is important for the present model family because it challenges a binary representation of authenticity and concealment. Students can regulate exposure in degrees, and the same student may choose different degrees across situations.

The term “masking” in the present study is an operational umbrella for the lower-visibility end of this behavioral continuum. It includes actions that reduce the public salience of ability, intensity, interests, or performance under the modeled payoff structure. It should not be treated as interchangeable with every established use of masking or camouflaging in clinical and identity literatures. Nor does lower visibility necessarily imply deception. Privacy, selective disclosure, politeness, strategic self-presentation, and temporary conformity can be adaptive. The theoretical issue is whether the option remains selectively chosen because it serves a current purpose or becomes persistent because the learner no longer obtains adequate evidence about alternatives.

## Social coping is diverse, measurable, and context-dependent

The social-coping literature expanded the stigma paradigm from an interpretive account into measurable strategy patterns. Swiatek (1995) developed a questionnaire to identify coping strategies used by highly gifted adolescents. Factor analysis yielded dimensions involving denial of giftedness, popularity and conformity, peer acceptance, fear of failure, and activity level. Subsequent work showed that social coping was not a single continuum from healthy to unhealthy. Strategies differed in their social purpose and psychological correlates: denying giftedness, helping others, increasing social interaction, and reducing the importance of popularity are not behaviorally or ethically equivalent.

Rudasill et al. (2007) examined the Social Coping Questionnaire with 600 gifted students in Grades 5-11. Their exploratory and confirmatory analyses supported six factors but also found differences in factor patterns across age and gender groups. This result matters substantively as well as psychometrically. It implies that the structure and meaning of social coping may vary across developmental and social positions. A parameter in a computational model should therefore not be translated directly into a universal psychological type such as “the risk-averse gifted child.”

Longitudinal evidence further shows that coping strategies can change with context. Cross and Swiatek (2009) followed approximately 300 academically gifted adolescents after entry into a residential academy. Across roughly the first year, denial of giftedness became more common, social interaction became less common, and reported peer acceptance increased. The directions are not reducible to a single improvement-or-deterioration narrative. Entry into a new high-ability environment changed several strategies at once, illustrating that students adapt to local opportunities, identities, and social goals.

Cross et al. (2019) extended the contextual argument across countries. Interviews with 90 gifted students from the United States, United Kingdom, South Korea, Ireland, and France found broad awareness of the visibility of exceptional ability, adult and peer expectations, jealousy, and possible rejection. Students described hiding ability, conforming, helping peers, and other strategies, but their use and meaning varied across cultures. The study supports the existence of a recurring visibility-management problem without establishing a culturally invariant response.

Taken together, this literature proves neither that masking is universal nor that it is always harmful. It establishes three narrower and more useful facts. First, some gifted students monitor how ability is perceived. Second, they possess multiple strategies for changing its visibility or social meaning. Third, strategy use varies across situations, groups, developmental stages, and cultures. These findings support a contextual learning model because they place behavioral choice between the student and the environment rather than solely inside either one.

## Existing evidence describes strategies better than it explains persistence

The giftedness literature is strongest at identifying perceived stigma, cataloguing coping strategies, and comparing groups or settings. It is weaker at specifying the trial-by-trial process through which one strategy becomes entrenched. Much of the foundational evidence comes from interviews, scenarios, or questionnaires administered at one time. Longitudinal exceptions demonstrate that coping changes, but they do not usually record the sequence of visible action, peer response, subsequent choice, and changing context at the temporal resolution required to identify a learning rule.

This leaves several causal questions open. A student who conceals ability after rejection may be responding optimally to a persistently unreceptive environment. Another may be avoiding a currently receptive environment because earlier negative experiences discouraged further testing. A third may know that one group is welcoming but find it difficult to identify that group among many alternatives. A fourth may face genuine acceptance yet still experience a comparison-based cost to self-concept. Cross-sectional observations of “masking” cannot distinguish these mechanisms because they can produce the same visible endpoint.

The literature also rarely compares observed behavior with an explicit benchmark for what would be optimal under the assumed environment. Without such a benchmark, high masking can be described as frequent or persistent but not as suboptimal. That distinction is central. If masking maximizes expected long-run value, it is an adaptive response to the modeled contingencies. If authenticity maximizes value but masking persists, the explanation must involve learning history, restricted information, action representation, or another failure to discover the better policy.

## Hot-stove learning supplies the missing sequential mechanism

March (1996) challenged trait explanations of apparent risk aversion by showing how adaptive learning can generate conservative behavior. When risky alternatives produce variable outcomes, early negative samples can lower their estimated value. Learners then select them less often, while safer alternatives accumulate more stable evidence. Observed avoidance can consequently emerge even when no fixed aversion to risk was specified.

Denrell and March (2001) formulated this process as adaptation through information restriction. Adaptive systems preferentially reproduce actions that have performed well, but this sensible rule changes the sample from which later beliefs are formed. An alternative that initially appears poor is sampled less; because it is sampled less, its estimate receives fewer opportunities for correction. Risky and novel alternatives are especially vulnerable because a small number of adverse outcomes can terminate exploration before their favorable tail is adequately observed.

This hot-stove mechanism fits the giftedness problem closely. Visible authenticity may be valuable on average and may eventually enable compatible friendships, recognition, or intellectual engagement. Yet any single attempt can produce rejection, jealousy, ridicule, unwanted attention, or pressure. Masking may carry an ongoing cost but produce a predictable short-term social return. If one rejected attempt sharply reduces subsequent visibility, the student receives less evidence about whether peers have changed, whether another form of participation would succeed, or whether a different context is receptive. Avoidance then preserves the pessimistic estimate that generated it.

Reinforcement learning provides a formal language for this process because it separates three components: the objective payoff structure, the subjective impact of an experienced outcome, and the rule that converts learned value into future sampling (Sutton and Barto, 2018). This separation prevents several conceptual conflations. A severe rejection penalty is a property of the modeled environment; greater weighting of the same negative outcome is a learner parameter; and low exploration is a property of action selection. Each can increase masking, but they imply different empirical predictions and different forms of support.

The hot-stove account also makes a stronger claim than simple reinforcement. It is not merely that punished behavior decreases. The critical mechanism is that decreased behavior reduces access to information. Persistent masking can therefore survive after authenticity becomes objectively favorable, because the learner does not automatically receive counterfactual evidence about an action it no longer selects. This is the gap the M0 and M2 models are designed to expose.

## Graded disclosure links visibility management to exploratory opportunity

The visibility-management literature suggests that gifted students do not face only a binary choice. Placating, partial participation, humor, helping, selective truthfulness, and context-specific disclosure can all regulate exposure (Cross et al., 2014; Cross et al., 2019). These strategies differ in content and social meaning, but they share a structural feature: they can make the learner visible without assuming the full risk of maximal disclosure.

This observation motivates M1. In learning terms, a graded action may be valuable even when it is not the best final policy. It can preserve contact with informative outcomes. A low-stakes contribution, a partial expression of an unusual interest, or disclosure to a limited audience may reveal whether the environment is changing while limiting the consequences of failure. This is an exploratory value, not necessarily an objective payoff advantage.

The existing literature does not yet establish a general causal ladder from small successful disclosures to full authenticity. Questionnaire factors do not specify whether learning transfers between neighboring actions, and the same outward behavior may serve different goals. An empirical test would need repeated observations of graded participation, perceived response, subsequent participation, and generalization across similar settings. The model therefore converts a documented continuum of visibility into a precise hypothesis: lower-exposure actions may prevent exploration from collapsing after a negative outcome.

## Peer context changes both acceptance and the cost of authenticity

Peer relationships are not a single stable environment. The findings of Verschueren et al. (2019) show that high-ability students' perceived acceptance varies with classroom composition. Cross et al. (2019) show that visibility concerns and coping differ across cultures. Cross and Swiatek (2009) show that movement into a residential high-ability setting changes coping over time. Together, these studies imply that a student may encounter several “ponds” with different norms, comparison standards, and probabilities of acceptance.

This creates a search problem largely absent from static accounts of person-environment fit. It is not enough for a receptive group to exist. The learner must identify it, distinguish it from unreceptive alternatives, and sample authentic behavior there often enough to learn its value. When safety information is context-specific, adding social options can expand opportunity and simultaneously increase the burden of discovering which opportunity is good. The M3 model isolates that distinction by comparing action representations in which context search is preserved, compressed, or removed.

Context also changes the value of acceptance itself. The big-fish-little-pond effect describes a negative association between the achievement level of the comparison group and an individual's academic self-concept after individual achievement is controlled. Marsh and Hau (2003) demonstrated the effect across 26 countries. Fang et al. (2018), synthesizing 33 studies and 56 effect sizes with more than 1.27 million students, reported a mean effect of approximately \(\beta=-0.28\), with variation by age and other moderators.

Research focused on gifted grouping shows why this mechanism requires careful treatment. Preckel et al. (2010) found declining mathematics self-concept soon after entry into gifted classes, consistent with a changing reference group, while also finding that the setting provided more appropriate challenge. In a later four-wave study, Preckel et al. (2019) found achievement benefits from full-time high-ability grouping without corresponding academic self-concept costs in the studied sample. These results are not contradictory so much as boundary-setting: grouping can alter comparison, challenge, belonging, and achievement simultaneously, and its net consequences depend on design and context.

The comparison-cost component of M3 should therefore be understood as a theoretical counterforce, not a calibrated model of gifted placement. A receptive high-ability context may improve peer fit while also intensifying self-comparison. The model asks whether a sufficiently large comparison cost can offset the social reward of acceptance. It does not estimate the size of that cost in students or imply that selective grouping is generally harmful.

## Stigma, context, and behavior can create temporal dependence

The literature reviewed above suggests several ways in which history can matter. A rejected display may alter what peers expect; a gifted label may change how later behavior is interpreted; entry into a new school may reset some relationships while preserving the learner's prior beliefs; and repeated concealment may reduce opportunities to form relationships organized around authentic interests. These possibilities are widely plausible, but the empirical literature seldom distinguishes a persistent state of the environment from a persistent learned belief about the environment.

M2 and M4 separate those ideas. M2 represents stigma and acceptance as explicit environmental states. It asks whether rejection changes future payoffs enough to make masking structurally optimal, and it compares that optimal policy with what a Q-learner actually learns. M4 instead represents receptivity as a continuous outcome partly shaped by prior behavior. It asks whether masking can become self-reinforcing because reduced visibility weakens the future conditions under which authenticity would succeed.

These formulations extend the literature without claiming that peer systems literally follow the model equations. Their contribution is conceptual precision. “Persistence” can refer to at least four different phenomena: continued avoidance under a fixed environment; rational adaptation to a lasting adverse state; stochastic path dependence caused by coupled learning and feedback; or genuine hysteresis demonstrated by different transition thresholds during parameter reversal. The existing evidence on social coping supports the relevance of history, but it does not by itself establish which of these processes operates in human students.

## Synthesis and research gap

The empirical literature supports a strong but bounded premise. Gifted students are not a socially deficient population, yet some encounter settings in which exceptional ability becomes socially consequential. They monitor visibility and use diverse strategies to influence how peers respond. Those strategies vary across age, gender, culture, classroom composition, and educational placement. Academic self-concept is also shaped by the achievement level of the comparison group, so a context can be receptive in one sense and costly in another.

What remains underdeveloped is a sequential account of persistence. Existing studies describe what students report doing and how strategy patterns differ across groups, but they rarely identify whether masking persists because the environment remains hostile, because early experience restricted later evidence, because safer intermediate actions were unavailable, because a receptive context was difficult to find, because comparison reduced its value, or because prior masking altered future receptivity. These mechanisms have different educational implications despite producing similar observed behavior.

The present model cascade is designed around that gap. M0 formalizes hot-stove information restriction in the smallest possible choice problem. M1 converts the literature's continuum of visibility into graded exploratory actions. M2 adds accepted and stigmatized states and benchmarks learning against the optimal dynamic policy. M3 turns heterogeneous peer environments into a context-search problem and adds an explicit comparison cost. M4 allows behavior and receptivity to co-evolve. The models do not claim that one simulated mechanism explains all masking. They provide discriminable candidate explanations and show what empirical sequence data would be needed to test them.

The resulting research program is therefore longitudinal and contextual. It should measure visible participation, perceived and observed peer response, subsequent sampling of similar actions, movement across contexts, and changes in perceived receptivity. It should distinguish strategic privacy from unwanted concealment and ask whether low-visibility behavior remains adaptive under current conditions. Only such data can determine when masking reflects accurate environmental learning, when it reflects information restriction, and when changing the environment rather than the student is the appropriate target.
