## Submission scope statement

This Original Research article fits the Educational Psychology section because it develops a transparent computational account of how repeated peer feedback may shape visible academic self-presentation. Research on gifted students documents context-dependent management of ability information, but existing work is primarily descriptive. The manuscript formalizes candidate learning mechanisms using a family of reinforcement-learning models and separates risky authenticity, graded disclosure, stigma-state transitions, search across peer contexts, social-comparison cost, and endogenous peer receptivity. It combines analytic reward thresholds, Monte Carlo summaries, a value-iteration benchmark, action-space ablations, and an ascending-descending continuation test. The educational contribution is not a claim that gifted students share a deficit or that the simulations establish intervention effects. Instead, the models yield testable predictions about when a learning environment may stabilize reduced visibility, when fresh-start opportunities alter behavior, and when clear signals about receptive contexts reduce search burden. The paper also demonstrates why superficially similar masking rates can arise from objectively unfavorable conditions, suboptimal experiential learning, context-search architecture, or feedback between behavior and peer response. These distinctions are directly relevant to educational research on classroom participation, peer relations, academic self-concept, and gifted education.
